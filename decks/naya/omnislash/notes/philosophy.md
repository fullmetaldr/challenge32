# Omnislash — Deck Philosophy

This note records the durable design philosophy for **Omnislash**, the Naya Commander deck led by **Cloud, Ex-SOLDIER**. It is intentionally not a changelog, upgrade queue, or list of cards under consideration. Individual card choices can change; the principles below should remain the standard against which those changes are judged.

## Core doctrine

> **An attack should never be just an attack.**

Omnislash wants to attack every turn with equipped creatures: ideally Cloud plus an armed supporting cast. Combat damage by itself is not enough. Each attack should create additional strategic value by drawing cards, generating resources, spreading damage, burning opponents or creatures, creating more combat opportunities, or otherwise advancing the deck toward a finish.

Pure combat and commander damage remain available as fallback plans, but they are not the deck's primary identity. Winning by simply making one creature enormous, finding evasion, and attacking the same opponent repeatedly is too slow, attracts too much attention, and is not interesting enough for the deck's intended play pattern.

## Early-game development

The first turns should be spent building the launchpad for Cloud rather than waiting for the commander to make the deck functional.

Turns 1–4 should prioritise a mixture of:

- **Early ramp**, with enough acceleration to bring a five-mana commander online ahead of curve.
- **Engine pieces** that will turn later combats into cards, mana, damage, or other value.
- **Protection**, preferably established before Cloud arrives so the first important combat step is difficult to disrupt.
- **At least one useful Equipment**, so Cloud can immediately begin the deck's normal play loop.
- **Cheap supporting bodies**, either as Equipment carriers or utility creatures that advance the engine.

### Cloud deployment target

**Turn 5 is the latest acceptable Cloud deployment, not the target.**

The deck should be built and mulliganed with the expectation that Cloud can regularly arrive on turn 3 or 4. Waiting until turn 5 before the main engine begins is too slow for the power level Omnislash is meant to occupy; by that point many games may have only a few meaningful turns remaining.

Early ramp therefore has unusually high strategic importance. Acceleration that helps cast Cloud ahead of schedule is more valuable than ramp that only becomes available after the combat engine is already functioning.

## Equipment philosophy

Omnislash is fundamentally an Equipment deck and should maintain a high density of Equipment. Equipment slots should not be surrendered casually, even for individually powerful cards, because drawing and deploying weapons consistently is part of the deck's structural reliability.

Each Equipment should ideally provide **strategic value beyond raw stats**. Power and toughness bonuses are welcome, but the preferred weapons also contribute one or more of the following:

- card advantage;
- protection or resilience;
- evasion;
- mana or resource generation;
- additional combat value;
- damage multiplication or spreading;
- interaction;
- a route toward a finisher or combo.

A weapon that only makes Cloud larger must be exceptionally efficient to justify its slot.

### Cards over fixed Treasure output

Cloud's Treasure production is useful, particularly for accelerating the midgame, but it is not the primary optimisation target. The Treasure reward does not scale with the number of equipped attackers in the same way Cloud's card draw does.

**Repeatable card draw is the higher priority.**

The more cards each combat produces, the more likely Omnislash is to find additional Equipment, protection, damage engines, finishers, and combo pieces. Equipping a supporting cast should therefore be rewarded rather than concentrating every weapon on Cloud simply to maximise his power.

## Resilience philosophy

Omnislash does not need to make its entire battlefield indestructible or impossible to interact with.

The minimum resilient state is:

> **Cloud + one meaningful Equipment.**

If the deck can reliably preserve, recover, or redeploy that state, it can resume attacking and rebuild through card draw. Protection should therefore focus on preventing the catastrophic state where Cloud is present but the arsenal has disappeared completely, rather than trying to protect every permanent at any cost.

Redundancy is valuable. The deck should contain multiple ways to perform important jobs so that losing one specific engine, weapon, or protection piece does not disable the strategy.

## Combat as an engine

Omnislash should favour effects that make attacks scale into something larger than combat damage.

Useful attack payoffs include:

- drawing additional cards for equipped attackers;
- spreading combat damage across multiple opponents;
- converting combat into direct burn;
- producing mana or other resources;
- creating bodies or additional armed attackers;
- untapping resources;
- generating additional combat steps;
- enabling explosive finishing turns.

The ideal board rewards attacking with several equipped creatures, so the supporting cast should be meaningful participants rather than passive utility pieces whenever possible.

## Win conditions

Omnislash should be capable of winning through several distinct but complementary routes.

### 1. Accumulated pressure

Repeated attacks should chip away at the entire table through combat plus collateral damage, burn, and scaling value. The goal is not merely to reduce one player's life total at a time; each combat should push multiple opponents closer to death while improving Omnislash's resources.

### 2. Explosive finishers

The deck should retain access to dramatic, high-impact plays capable of converting a developed board into a table kill in a single turn. These cards may be expensive or politically dangerous if their ceiling genuinely justifies them: once deployed, they should represent an intentional **"end the game now"** moment.

### 3. Combo and semi-combo finishes

Omnislash may use extra-combat, repeated-turn, or other synergistic lines that turn its established engines into a deterministic or near-deterministic finish. These lines should complement the normal combat plan rather than requiring the deck to become a dedicated combo shell, and they must remain compatible with the deck's Bracket 3 constraint.

## Power target

Omnislash is intended to be the **strongest possible technically defensible Bracket 3 deck**.

It does not need to be gentle within that bracket. The goal is to optimise aggressively for speed, resilience, interaction, consistency, and finishing power while remaining within the applicable Bracket 3 restrictions.

This is the flagship deck of the Challenge 32 project: it should be capable of sitting down at a strong Bracket 3 table and functioning as the deck to bring out when maximum pressure is appropriate.

Bracket definitions can change over time. When external Commander bracket rules change, individual card choices and combo lines should be re-evaluated while preserving this underlying target: **push the top edge of Bracket 3 without deliberately crossing into Bracket 4.**

## Evaluation rules for future changes

Future additions and cuts should be judged against the deck's core questions:

1. **Does this help Cloud come online earlier or more reliably?**
2. **Does this help ensure Cloud and the supporting cast have meaningful Equipment?**
3. **Does this make an attack do more than simply deal combat damage?**
4. **Does this improve card flow, resilience, interaction, or recovery?**
5. **Does this help convert accumulated advantage into a real win?**
6. **Does the change preserve enough Equipment density and functional redundancy?**
7. **Does the deck remain technically defensible as Bracket 3?**

A card does not need to satisfy every question, but cards that satisfy none of them should need an exceptional reason to remain.
